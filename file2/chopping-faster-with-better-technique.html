Chopping Faster With Better Technique

A sharp knife is safer than a dull one, because a dull blade slips where a sharp one bites.

By Daily Ledger Desk October 28, 2026 2 min read

Technique matters more than expensive equipment.

This guide stays practical on purpose. Everything below is something you can act on this week without specialist equipment or a large budget.

Why it matters

It is worth understanding why these habits work before trying to follow them, because the reasoning is what makes them stick.

A sharp blade requires less pressure, which means less slipping and less fatigue.

Efficient preparation shortens the total cooking time, which is what stops home cooks ordering takeaway on tired evenings.

A stable board and a claw grip protect fingers far better than concentration alone.

What to expect

In real use, the change is usually smaller and more manageable than people expect.

One good chef knife outperforms a drawer of specialised blades that never get used.

Cutting on glass or stone destroys an edge quickly, so a wooden or plastic board is worth having.

The short version:

  • Review after a couple of weeks and keep only what earned its place.
  • Start smaller than feels impressive, then add to it only once it is easy.
  • Remove friction where you can, because willpower is the least reliable tool available.
  • Track one simple measure so you can tell whether a change is actually working.

Step by step

Follow these in order. Each step assumes the one before it, so there is little value in jumping ahead.

  1. Set up a stable board on a damp cloth and clear the area around it.
  2. Adopt the claw grip and place the knuckles against the side of the blade.
  3. Start with the rocking motion for herbs and garlic, keeping the tip on the board.
  4. Practise even slices on a low pressure ingredient before moving to anything expensive.
  5. Hone the knife, then store it on a magnetic strip or in a guard rather than loose in a drawer.

Practical tips

  • Curl your fingertips under so the flat of the blade rides against your knuckles.
  • Cut larger items into stable flat surfaces first so they cannot roll.
  • Sharpen periodically with a whetstone or a professional service.
  • Wash and dry knives immediately rather than leaving them in a sink.
  • Let the weight of the knife do the work rather than pressing down hard.

Frequently asked

How often should I sharpen my knife?

Honing before use keeps the edge aligned for daily work, while actual sharpening is needed far less often, depending on how much you cook and what you cut.

Is one good knife enough?

For most home cooking, yes. A medium chef knife plus a small paring knife covers nearly everything, and both will get used more than a full set.

Are expensive knives worth it?

Beyond a reasonable quality tier, you are paying for balance and materials rather than performance. A well maintained mid range knife outperforms an expensive neglected one.

Why does my knife slip on tomatoes?

The blade is blunt. A sharp edge bites into the skin immediately, which is also why sharpening makes the kitchen safer overall.

The goal is a routine that runs without much thought. Once something feels automatic, it stops being a decision and becomes simply how you do things.
