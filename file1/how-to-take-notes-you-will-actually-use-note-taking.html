How to Take Notes You Will Actually Use — Note taking

The value of a note appears at review time, not at the moment you write it.

By Daily Ledger Desk October 23, 2026 2 min read

Notes written to be reread are far less useful than notes written to be rebuilt from memory later.

The aim here is a clear starting point: what to do first, what to skip, and how to tell whether it is working.

Why it matters

Once the underlying reason is clear, most of the individual tips stop feeling arbitrary.

Condensing material into your own words requires understanding, which is exactly what creates durable memory.

Organised notes with clear headings can be scanned in minutes during revision instead of reread in full.

Attempting to recall before looking at notes is one of the strongest study techniques available, and it needs good notes to work against.

What to expect

In real use, the change is usually smaller and more manageable than people expect.

A workable system separates capturing from processing: get the idea down quickly, then clean it up shortly afterwards.

One place for all notes matters more than the specific application, because scattered notes are never reviewed.

The short version:

  • Consistency matters more than intensity in almost every case.
  • Review after a couple of weeks and keep only what earned its place.
  • Remove friction where you can, because willpower is the least reliable tool available.
  • Track one simple measure so you can tell whether a change is actually working.

Step by step

The sequence matters more than the speed, so take these at a pace you can sustain.

  1. Choose one tool and one location where every note will live.
  2. Capture quickly during the session, accepting imperfect writing.
  3. Rewrite or tidy the notes within a day, cutting anything redundant.
  4. Add headings, a short summary and questions you can use for self testing.
  5. Schedule brief recall reviews at increasing intervals rather than rereading immediately.

Practical tips

  • Turn headings into questions so you can test yourself during review.
  • Add a one line summary at the top of each note stating what it is for.
  • Clean up and merge rough notes within a day while the context is still fresh.
  • Keep one master structure for every subject rather than inventing a new layout each time.
  • Review using recall first, then check the notes to see what you missed.

Common questions

Handwriting or typing?

Typing is faster and easier to search; handwriting tends to force more summarising because it is slower. Choose the one you will consistently review rather than the theoretically superior option.

How detailed should notes be?

Detailed enough that you can reconstruct the idea later without the source, and no more. Excess detail makes review slower without improving recall.

Should I reread my notes before a test?

Rereading is the least effective option. Testing yourself from the notes, or from the headings alone, produces far stronger recall in the same time.

What if I miss part of a lecture?

Mark the gap clearly, get the missing section from a peer or the recording, and fill it in while the surrounding context still makes sense.

Start with the step that solves the most annoying part of your current situation. Momentum from one visible win makes everything that follows considerably easier.
