Small Space Composting That Does Not Smell

A working compost heap is a simple system: add, turn occasionally, and let time do the rest.

By Daily Ledger Desk October 24, 2026 2 min read

Even a balcony can process a meaningful share of household food scraps.

None of this requires prior experience. The steps are ordered so that each one makes the next easier rather than harder.

Why it matters

It is worth understanding why these habits work before trying to follow them, because the reasoning is what makes them stick.

A compost system makes you more aware of what you throw away, which tends to reduce waste overall.

Finished compost improves soil structure and helps it hold moisture, which reduces watering.

Organic waste broken down at home never needs collecting, transported or processed elsewhere.

What to expect

In real use, the change is usually smaller and more manageable than people expect.

Green material such as fruit and vegetable scraps supplies nitrogen, while brown material such as cardboard and dry leaves supplies carbon.

Aim for roughly equal volumes of green and brown, with brown slightly dominant to keep airflow and prevent sogginess.

The short version:

  • Track one simple measure so you can tell whether a change is actually working.
  • Start smaller than feels impressive, then add to it only once it is easy.
  • Consistency matters more than intensity in almost every case.
  • Review after a couple of weeks and keep only what earned its place.

Step by step

Work through them one at a time and give each a few days before moving on.

  1. Choose a spot with decent drainage and partial shade, or a bin that fits a balcony.
  2. Start with a layer of coarse brown material at the base for airflow.
  3. Alternate green and brown additions with each load rather than dumping one type at once.
  4. Keep the pile as damp as a wrung out sponge, adding water or dry material as needed.
  5. Turn it periodically and check that it is warming slightly, which shows activity is happening.

Practical tips

  • Add a handful of finished compost or garden soil to introduce the microorganisms that start the process.
  • Harvest from the bottom, where the material has had the longest to break down.
  • Avoid meat, fish and dairy in a basic home system, since they attract pests and slow the balance.
  • Turn the pile every week or two if you want faster results, or leave it if you are happy to wait.
  • Chop larger scraps smaller to speed up decomposition noticeably.

Common questions

How long does compost take?

Anywhere from a couple of months to a year, depending on size, balance, moisture and how often it is turned. Chopping scraps and turning more often shortens it.

What if I do not have a garden?

Finished compost still has a use: community gardens, planters on a balcony or sharing it with neighbours all make use of it, and the waste reduction benefit happens either way.

Why does my compost smell?

Almost always too much wet green material without enough brown, or a lack of air. Add shredded cardboard, turn the pile and the odour normally clears quickly.

Can I compost in a flat?

Yes, with a sealed bokashi system or a small worm bin designed for indoors. Both handle food scraps without the space or smell of an outdoor heap.

Progress here comes from repetition rather than intensity. A modest change you keep will always beat an ambitious one you abandon after a few days.
